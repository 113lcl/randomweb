How we run a 14-person studio across six timezones.

Noah Bergström
Strategy Director

On this page

𝕏 in

Pulsar's fourteen people work from Warsaw, Lisbon, Cape Town, Toronto, São Paulo and Tokyo. On paper, that sounds like a coordination nightmare. In practice, it's forced us to build habits that make the studio run better than it did when we were four people in one Warsaw apartment.

Designing for four hours of overlap

We protect a single four-hour daily window where every timezone can plausibly be online, reserved exclusively for things that truly need real-time discussion — client calls, design crits that benefit from live reaction, and onboarding new hires. Everything else is explicitly built to not need that window, which turns out to be almost all of the actual work.

Writing over meetings

The habit that changed the most for us was defaulting to writing. A decision that would have been a 30-minute call two years ago is now a written proposal with context, options and a recommendation, posted somewhere everyone can respond to on their own schedule. It's slower to get a first reaction, but faster to get to a well-reasoned decision, because people respond after actually thinking rather than reacting live in a room.

Async isn't slower by default. Badly run synchronous work is slower — it just hides the cost inside everyone's calendar instead of a document.

Asynchronous critique

Design critique was the hardest habit to move async, since so much of design feedback culture assumes a room and a projector. Our version now: designers record a two-to-four minute Loom walking through their work and open questions, post it with the file, and teammates leave timestamped comments over the following 24 hours. It's slower per round, but the feedback is more considered, and quieter teammates — who often say less in a live crit — contribute as much as anyone.

The handoff ritual

Every design-to-engineering handoff follows the same written template: what changed, why, what's explicitly out of scope, and a Loom of the interaction states that are hard to convey in a static file. This one habit alone cut our "wait, is this supposed to do that?" Slack threads by more than half, because the answer to almost every ambiguity question is already written down before anyone has to ask.

None of this is exotic. It's mostly discipline — writing things down a little more carefully than feels necessary in the moment, so that the studio can function without ever requiring everyone to be awake at once.
