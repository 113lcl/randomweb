The case for fewer, bolder typefaces in brand systems.

Kenji Watanabe
Lead Brand Designer

On this page

𝕏 in

Open ten random brand guideline PDFs and you'll find, on average, four to six typefaces: a display face, a body face, a "friendly" alternate for marketing, a monospace for stats, sometimes a script for "special moments." Almost none of these systems feel more distinctive for having more typefaces. Most feel less coherent.

The type-library trap

More typefaces feel like more options, and more options feel like more expressiveness. In practice, the opposite tends to happen: with six typefaces available, every designer on a team reaches for a different one in a different context, and the brand stops looking like it belongs to a single voice. The type choices become a symptom of who touched the file last, not a deliberate expression of the brand.

What constraint buys you

Our default recommendation to clients is two typefaces — sometimes just one, used at different weights and sizes to do all the work. This isn't minimalism for its own sake. A tight type system forces every remaining decision to matter more: weight, size, spacing and color become the primary tools for hierarchy and mood, rather than typeface-switching papering over unclear structure.

Distinctiveness comes from doing a small number of things with total conviction, not from having an answer for every possible context.

It also makes a brand dramatically easier to maintain. A marketing team briefing a freelance designer, or an engineer implementing a new screen, has one obvious set of type decisions to reach for — not six options to reconcile against "brand feel."

Choosing a pairing

When we do use two typefaces, we look for maximum contrast in role, not aesthetic similarity: a distinctive, characterful display face used sparingly for headlines and moments of emphasis, paired with a highly legible, quiet workhorse for everything read at length. The goal is that nobody could mistake which face is doing which job, even glancing at a page from across a room.

One family, real range

Modern variable fonts have made single-family systems more viable than ever — a well-built variable typeface can cover display headlines, body copy, and UI labels through weight and optical-size axes alone, without ever introducing a second family. For several 2025 engagements, including Aurora's rebrand, we shipped identities built entirely on one variable font family, using weight and tracking as the only levers — and the result reads as more consistent, not less rich.
